3:T11eb,<p>Apple just dropped a research paper that should make you question everything you&#39;ve heard about AI reasoning.</p>
<p>It&#39;s called &quot;The Illusion of Thinking&quot;. The title is not subtle.</p>
<p>Six researchers tested the most advanced reasoning models available: OpenAI o1 and o3, DeepSeek R1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet Thinking, and Google Gemini Flash Thinking. They didn&#39;t use standard benchmarks. They used classic logic puzzles like the Tower of Hanoi, the river-crossing problem, and block-stacking challenges.</p>
<p>The results are brutal.</p>
<h2>Three performance regimes</h2>
<p>The researchers identified three distinct patterns:</p>
<p>Low complexity. Standard models without reasoning capabilities actually outperform the fancy reasoning models. The extra thinking gets in the way.</p>
<p>Medium complexity. This is where reasoning models shine. The additional thinking tokens provide a real advantage.</p>
<p>High complexity. Complete collapse. Both models fail entirely. Accuracy drops to zero.</p>
<p>The models don&#39;t just struggle. They give up.</p>
<h2>The counter-intuitive scaling limit</h2>
<p>Here&#39;s the strangest finding.</p>
<p>As problems get harder, reasoning models initially increase their effort. They use more tokens. They &quot;think&quot; harder.</p>
<p>But at a certain point, something flips. Despite having an adequate token budget, the models reduce their output. They stop trying.</p>
<p>The paper suggests this might be a form of internal uncertainty estimation. The model implicitly detects that the task is beyond its capabilities and abandons the effort.</p>
<h2>The Tower of Hanoi test</h2>
<p>Take the Tower of Hanoi puzzle. You have disks stacked on three pegs. The goal is to move all disks to the third peg without placing a larger disk on a smaller one.</p>
<p>It&#39;s a simple problem. Humans can solve it by following a recursive algorithm.</p>
<p>Claude 3.7 Sonnet and DeepSeek R1 start failing when you add a fifth disk. With eight disks, they collapse completely.</p>
<p>The models literally tell you they&#39;re stopping to save tokens.</p>
<h2>The rebuttal</h2>
<p>Not everyone agrees with Apple&#39;s framing.</p>
<p>Alex Lawsen from Open Philanthropy published a detailed rebuttal called &quot;The Illusion of the Illusion of Thinking&quot;. He argues that Apple&#39;s experimental design has problems.</p>
<p>First, token limits. At the point where Apple claims models collapse, models were hitting their output ceilings.</p>
<p>Second, impossible puzzles. Apple&#39;s river-crossing test included unsolvable instances. Models were penalized for recognizing that and refusing to solve them.</p>
<p>Third, rigid evaluation. Apple&#39;s automated scripts judged models solely by complete move lists, even when the task exceeded token limits.</p>
<p>Lawsen reran the tests. He asked models to generate recursive code that solves the puzzle instead of listing every move. The models had no trouble solving 15-disk Hanoi problems.</p>
<h2>The middle ground</h2>
<p>A subsequent paper from Spanish researchers tried to clarify the debate.</p>
<p>They replicated and refined both contested benchmarks. Their finding? Both sides have a point.</p>
<p>The Tower of Hanoi failures weren&#39;t purely about output constraints. LRMs genuinely struggle with cognitive limitations when complexity rises moderately.</p>
<p>But the river-crossing results were a different story. Once you limit tests to solvable problems, LRMs effortlessly solve large instances with over 100 agent pairs.</p>
<h2>What this means for you</h2>
<p>The debate matters for one simple reason: money.</p>
<p>Billions are being poured into reasoning models. Companies are betting that these systems can solve complex real-world problems.</p>
<p>Apple&#39;s research suggests there&#39;s a ceiling. Beyond a certain complexity, throwing more processing power at the problem doesn&#39;t help.</p>
<p>The practical takeaway? Focus AI on structured, low-to-mid complexity tasks. Don&#39;t expect it to formulate high-level strategy or solve chaotic real-world problems.</p>
<p>Use it to extract key points from contracts. Create summaries. Automate straightforward workflows. Leave the complex reasoning to humans.</p>
<p>The models are useful. But they&#39;re not thinking. They&#39;re pattern-matching at scale.</p>
<p>And once the puzzle gets hard enough, even the pattern-matching stops.</p>
